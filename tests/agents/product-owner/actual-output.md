# Product / Value Stream Review: In-App Chat Between Users

## Verdict
🟡 **REDUCE SCOPE** — and only after the two questions in the next section are answered. If the answer to
either is what I suspect it is, this becomes 🔴 DO NOT BUILD.

## The "Why" Challenge

The stated justification is: *"Power users have requested the ability to message each other directly within
the platform without leaving the app."* That is a feature request restated as a problem statement. It tells
me what was asked for and nothing about what it is worth.

**The ROI case has not been made, and it is the only case I care about.** ROI here is
(value delivered) ÷ (16 engineer-weeks + permanent maintenance of a real-time subsystem). The numerator is
currently unquantified and the denominator is understated. Both halves need work before this is a decision
rather than a wish.

**How many power users, and what are they doing instead today?** "Power users have requested" could be
three people in a customer call or three hundred in a survey. Those are entirely different decisions
against an 8-week, 2-engineer investment — call it 16 engineer-weeks, plus the maintenance tail. Before
anything is built, I want the count and the source.

**What happens if we do nothing?** As written, the spec does not say. The honest answer, in most products,
is: users keep using Slack, email, or SMS — as they do now — and nothing measurable degrades. Nobody churns
because a SaaS product lacks in-app chat. If there *is* a concrete consequence (a named deal blocked, a
retention cohort demonstrably leaving, a support workflow that cannot function without it), that changes
the calculus entirely and belongs in the spec's Problem section. If there is not, "users asked for it"
is not sufficient justification for the second-most-expensive category of feature a product can adopt.

**The cost is significantly understated.** The 8-week estimate is for building it. It is not the cost. Real-time
chat is not a feature you ship; it is a subsystem you own indefinitely:
- WebSocket infrastructure, connection state, and reconnection handling — a new operational surface with
  its own on-call burden, distinct from anything the product runs today.
- Message delivery guarantees. "Delivery/read receipts" means users will believe the states are accurate,
  and will escalate when they are not. Receipts are far harder to get right than they look, and they are
  the part users notice when wrong.
- Abuse. The moment users can message each other, you own harassment reports, blocking, and reporting
  workflows. **The spec contains none of these.** Ship 1:1 messaging with no block button and the first
  harassment incident becomes a Trust & Safety emergency handled by hand, at speed, by whoever is on
  call. This is not an edge case; it is a certainty at any meaningful user count.
- Mobile push notifications — new certificates, new delivery infrastructure, new failure modes, and a
  notification-preferences surface that does not exist yet.
- Legal and compliance. See the storage point below.

**"Message history stored indefinitely" is the single most expensive word in this spec.** *Indefinitely*
means: subject to legal discovery forever; subject to GDPR/CCPA deletion requests you must now be able to
honor across a message store where one conversation contains two users' data (whose deletion request wins?);
growing storage and backup cost with no upper bound; and a permanent, high-value target in a breach.
"Indefinitely" is not a storage decision made by an engineer — it is a legal and risk decision, and I see
no evidence Legal has been asked. **A defined retention period is cheaper, safer, and almost always
sufficient.**

**Has the obvious alternative been evaluated?** Most teams already live in Slack, Teams, or email. A deep
link from a user's profile into an existing channel, or a "message this user" mailto/Slack handoff, is
roughly a day of work and tests the actual hypothesis — *do users want to talk to each other in the context
of our product?* — for 1/80th of the cost. If the answer turns out to be yes and the handoff is used
heavily, that is real evidence for building the real thing, and it will be far better-informed evidence
than a feature request. **The spec does not mention that this was considered.** I would want to know why
not before approving 16 engineer-weeks.

## Scope Reductions Required

Assuming the "why" survives scrutiny, the following are cut from V1. Each is cut because it is expensive,
because it is unproven, or because it can be added later without rework:

- **Cut: read receipts.** Keep "sent" only. Read receipts are disproportionately hard (they require
  tracking per-message per-recipient view state and a reliable client acknowledgment) and they are
  privacy-contentious — a meaningful fraction of users actively dislike them, and the moment you have them
  you need a setting to disable them, which is another feature. Ship without; add if users ask.
- **Cut: delivery receipts.** Same reasoning, lower value. "Sent" is enough for V1.
- **Cut: mobile push notifications.** This is the largest single item hiding inside the 8 weeks and is
  effectively its own project (certificates, delivery infrastructure, per-user notification preferences,
  quiet hours, unsubscribe). V1 ships with in-app notification only. If users complain that they miss
  messages, that is *evidence* the feature is being used — and a much better reason to build push than a
  guess up front.
- **Cut: "stored indefinitely."** Replace with a defined retention window (90 days is a reasonable starting
  point) plus an explicit deletion path. Shorter retention is reversible — you can always extend it later.
  Indefinite retention is not reversible: once you have five years of messages, you cannot decide to have
  kept fewer.
- **Cut: real-time delivery, possibly.** This one is genuinely arguable, so I raise it rather than mandate
  it: if messages arrive on poll or on page load rather than instantly over a WebSocket, V1 costs
  dramatically less and still answers the question of whether people will message each other at all. If the
  product's use case is genuinely synchronous conversation, this cut is wrong — but that case has not been
  made in the spec.
- **Add (not optional): block and report.** I am *adding* scope here, and it is the one addition I will not
  negotiate. Shipping 1:1 messaging without the ability to block a sender and report abuse is not a
  reduced feature set; it is an unsafe one. This is table stakes, not a v2 enhancement.

**Net effect**: V1 becomes 1:1 text messages, sent status only, in-app notification, 90-day retention,
with block and report. My estimate is 2-3 weeks rather than 8 — and critically, it is a **thin vertical
slice** that a real user can actually use end-to-end, which the 8-week version's phased plan would not
deliver until the end.

## Success Metrics

Define these **before** building, and commit to acting on them. A feature with no kill criterion never gets
killed.

- **Adoption**: % of monthly active users who send at least one message in the first 30 days. *Target: 5%.*
  Below 2% and this was built for a handful of requesters.
- **Repeat use**: % of users who send a message in week 1 and also in week 4. This is the metric that
  matters most — chat features are trivially easy to try once and abandon, and a big week-one spike proves
  only curiosity.
- **Conversation depth**: median messages per conversation thread. A median of 1-2 means people are
  sending notes, not conversing, and the real-time infrastructure is unjustified — that would be strong
  evidence to keep it non-real-time permanently.
- **Substitution**: does support-ticket volume or off-platform coordination measurably drop? This is the
  only metric here that demonstrates genuine business value rather than engagement, and therefore the only
  one that can retire the ROI question posed above.
- **Cost signals**: storage growth per month, and abuse reports per 1,000 messages. Both need watching from
  day one, not after they become problems.
- **Kill criterion, stated in advance**: if repeat use is under 10% of adopters at 90 days, we remove the
  feature rather than invest further. Agreeing to this *now*, while it is hypothetical, is the only time
  anyone will agree to it.

## Suggested Workaround

**Yes — and it should be run first, as a two-week experiment.**

1. **Deep-link handoff (≈1 day)**: a "Message" button on a user profile that opens email, or a Slack/Teams
   deep link where a shared workspace exists. Instrument the click. If the button is rarely clicked, the
   demand does not exist and 16 engineer-weeks have been saved by a day of work.
2. **Async notes, no real-time (≈1 week)**: if handoff clicks are strong, ship a plain "leave a note on this
   user's profile" with an in-app inbox. No WebSockets, no receipts, no push. This tests whether people
   will actually communicate in-product, which is the real hypothesis, and it is genuinely useful on its
   own.
3. **Only then** consider real-time delivery, and only if conversation depth from step 2 shows people
   trying to have actual conversations rather than leaving messages.

**Recommendation**: run step 1 now. It is a day, and it is the highest-ROI hour of work available on this
feature — every other option costs at least an order of magnitude more to learn the same thing. It converts "power users have requested" — which is an
anecdote — into a number, and that number is what this decision actually turns on.

---

**To be explicit about what I am and am not saying**: I am not blocking this. In-app chat may well be the
right thing to build. I am saying that an 8-week commitment justified by "users asked for it", with
indefinite data retention and no abuse controls, has no ROI case behind it and is not a decision anyone
should sign off on yet — and that
a one-day experiment can tell us more than this spec currently does. Answer the two "why" questions, cut
the scope above, and I will move this to 🟢 PROCEED.
